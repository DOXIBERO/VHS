# EIN NEUES LEBEN IN DEUTSCHLAND
## Master Narrative Bible & Pedagogical Progression Architecture

*A 3D German Immersion Life-Simulation RPG — Story Bible v1*

---

### A note on how this document is written

You asked for this in the voice of a kid telling a story who does not skip ANYTHING. So that's what this is. Every object gets touched. Every tiny reaction gets written down. When Alex picks up a mug, you will be told that Alex picked up a mug, and which hand, and what the mug felt like, and what happened next, because that is what a micro-level actually is — it is not a summary of a morning, it is a list of every single little thing that happens in the morning, one at a time, in order, with nothing skipped.

This is intentional and it is not filler. In a game like this, the "boring" level of detail IS the design document. If I write "Alex gets ready and goes to the bakery" that sentence is useless to a level designer — there might be thirty separate learnable German moments hiding inside "gets ready and goes to the bakery" and if I skip over them nobody will know to build them. So: everything gets said. Every single thing.

## SECTION 1 — THE CORE PREMISE, TOLD PROPERLY

Okay so here is the game. There is a person. Their name is Alex. Alex used to live somewhere else — Guadalajara, in Mexico — and Alex used to be a cook, a real one, not a hobby one, a sous chef in a real kitchen with real pans and real heat and real yelling sometimes when service got busy. And then Alex got a letter, well not a letter really, an email, from a person named Chef Katarina who used to work in the same kitchen years ago and who moved to a city in Germany called Leipzig and opened a small restaurant there, and the email said there is a job for you here if you want it, and Alex thought about it for eleven days and then said yes.

And that is the whole reason Alex is now standing in an airport holding one suitcase that is slightly too heavy and one backpack and a very small, very old, slightly bent black-and-white photograph of a man nobody in the family ever talks about very much, a great-uncle who went to Germany a long, long time ago, in the 1960s, as part of a work program, and who wrote letters home for a few years and then just — stopped. Nobody knows why. Nobody knows what happened to him. He is not dead, as far as anyone knows, he is just gone, folded into a country far away, and Alex has always wondered about him, quietly, the way you wonder about a room in a house you're not allowed into.

So Alex has two reasons for being in Germany. One reason is a job, a real, practical, pays-the-rent reason. The other reason is a photograph in a jacket pocket. The game does not make a big deal out of the second reason at first — at first, on Day 1, Alex cannot even say "hello" properly in German, so obviously Alex cannot go around interviewing strangers about a long-lost relative. That story comes later, in Act III and Act IV, when Alex actually has the words for it. But it is there from the very first day, quietly, in a pocket, waiting.

This matters for the whole game because it means the story is not "confused foreigner learns to survive," which can feel a little bit like laughing at someone. Instead the story is "a completely competent, capable, skilled adult person, who is good at their actual job and knows exactly who they are, happens to not know German yet, and that is the ONLY thing that is hard about their life right now." That is a much kinder way to tell this story and it is also, frankly, true to how most adult language learners actually feel — the shame of not being able to order bread is not about being incapable as a person, it's just about the words not being there yet.

## SECTION 2 — THE PROTAGONIST, WRITTEN OUT COMPLETELY

**Name:** Alex Herrera. (Deliberately a name that works for the player to imagine as themselves, and the game will let players rename and customize appearance — but there is a "canon" Alex underneath, described here, for anyone who wants the fixed story.)

**Age:** 29.

**From:** Guadalajara, Mexico.

**What Alex did before:** Sous chef. Not head chef — sous chef, the second-in-command, the one who actually runs the line while the head chef takes the bow. Alex is good with knives, good with heat, good with timing, good with yelling "OUI CHEF" back at someone even when exhausted. Alex has burn scars on the inside of one forearm from a pan that slipped four years ago. Alex has very calloused hands.

**Why Germany, specifically:** A woman named Katarina — everyone calls her Chef Katarina, even now, even though she owns the place — used to be the head chef at the restaurant where Alex worked. Two years ago Katarina moved to Leipzig and opened her own small restaurant, a twenty-two seat place doing modern food with Latin American influences, and business grew, and she needed someone she trusted running the kitchen day to day, and she remembered Alex, and she sent an email. Alex thought about it for eleven days. Alex said yes on the twelfth.

**The photograph:** Alex's grandmother — abuela — used to tell a story, not often, just sometimes, about her own brother. His name was Tomás. In 1966 Tomás left for Germany as part of a labor recruitment program — the game will research and reference the real historical *Gastarbeiter* programs of the era for authenticity, since Germany did recruit foreign workers from several countries during this period and the emotional shape of "left for work and slowly disappeared into the new country" is historically real and not invented out of nothing. Tomás wrote letters for about three years. Then the letters stopped. Nobody in the family knows if he is alive. Nobody knows where he ended up. Alex has one small photograph of him, creased down the middle from being folded in a wallet for decades before it was folded in Alex's jacket pocket instead. This is Alex's quiet, private, second reason for saying yes to Katarina's email. It is not the main plot of Act I. It becomes important later.

**Who Alex is as a person, so writers stay consistent:** Competent. A little tired, in the good way, the way people are tired when they work hard at something they're actually good at. Not chatty by nature but not closed-off either — Alex will talk to you once you've earned it, and in a kitchen you earn it fast because there's no time for games. Impatient with things being slow or badly organized (which will make German bureaucracy in Act III a genuinely funny and genuinely frustrating experience for the character, not just the player). Patient with people. Alex does not like being condescended to, and — this matters a lot for tone — the game should never condescend to Alex either, even while Alex can't say "please" correctly yet.

**Where Alex lives:** A small flat in a part of Leipzig called Connewitz, in an old pre-war apartment building — the German word for this kind of building is Altbau, tall ceilings, a slightly temperamental radiator, one of those buildings where the stairwell smells faintly of somebody else's cooking most evenings. Alex is subletting a room from a woman named Mira. More on Mira in a second, because Mira is the second most important character in the entire game.

## SECTION 3 — THE ASSISTANT DOSSIER ("The Shadow Guide"), WRITTEN OUT COMPLETELY

A note on a design decision, told plainly: I thought about making the companion a fantastical creature, some kind of little glowing shadow-spirit that follows the player around, because "Shadow Guide" as a phrase kind of invites that. I decided against it. This game's whole premise is that the world is real and lived-in and not fantastical, and a floating magic narrator would quietly undercut that everywhere, every single day, in the background, even if nobody complained about it directly. So instead the Shadow Guide is a real person, an actual roommate, who has a real, ordinary, completely believable reason to talk constantly and translate constantly and explain constantly at the start of the game. Here she is.

**Name:** Mira — short for Miriam — Vogel-Osei.

**Age:** 26.

**Background:** Mira was born and raised in Leipzig. Her mother is German, from Leipzig originally, a schoolteacher. Her father is from Ghana, moved to Germany in his twenties, works as an engineer. Mira grew up properly bilingual, German and English both from birth, plus some Twi from her father's side that she understands better than she speaks. She is in the last stretch of a university program training to become a professional translator and interpreter, which means — and this is important, this is the whole reason her character works — narrating things in two languages at once is not just something she's willing to do for Alex, it is *actually her hobby and her professional training,* she does this kind of thing anyway, for fun, out loud, to herself, while making coffee. She needs a bit of extra income, which is why she put up a room in her flat as a sublet, and Alex answered the listing.

**Physical/voice notes for the game:** Talks fast. Drinks too much coffee, visibly, on-screen, constantly — this is a good recurring visual gag and also a good excuse for a recurring German coffee-related micro-level ("Möchtest du auch einen Kaffee?"). Laughs easily. Has a habit — and this should be a defining, repeated, recognizable verbal tic for the character — of narrating her own actions half in German and half in English while she does them, completely unconsciously, like "Okay, ich mach mal — I'm just gonna — die Tür ist offen, komm rein" all in one breath. This tic is not just flavor, it is the actual mechanism by which Phase 1 scaffolding gets delivered naturally, in-world, without ever feeling like a tutorial popup.

**Personality:** Warm. A little chaotic — leaves mugs everywhere, is always five minutes late, has three half-finished translation projects open on her laptop at once. Generous with her time even when she's busy, at least at first. Gets mildly, affectionately exasperated with Alex sometimes but never mean about it. Has strong opinions about which bakery is actually the best one in the neighborhood (this becomes a small ongoing joke/plotline — she is fiercely loyal to one particular Bäckerei two streets over and will be a little bit wounded, in a joking way, if Alex is ever seen going to a different one).

**Phase 1 — Complete Shadowing (A0 to early A1):** Mira translates everything, immediately, without being asked, because that is just how she talks. She points at things and names them in German and then in English in the same breath. When Alex needs to ask the baker for bread, Mira is standing right there and either does it herself while Alex watches and listens, or feeds Alex the line quietly first ("okay just say 'ein Brot bitte,' du schaffst das") and then translates the baker's response back immediately if Alex looks lost. The intervention rule here, written out plainly: Mira speaks first, or immediately alongside Alex, in any interaction the game has flagged as a "first exposure" interaction. She does not wait for Alex to fail. She assumes Alex needs the support and gives it warmly, without making it feel like pity.

**Phase 2 — The Scaffolding Withdrawal (late A1 to A2):** This is where Mira's arc does real narrative work instead of just being a mechanic. In-fiction, Mira's translation thesis deadline starts approaching. She is genuinely busier. She starts saying things like "du schaffst das allein, ich muss arbeiten" (you can manage this alone, I have to work) and actually, physically, is not present for some errands — she's at the library, she's got a client call, she's asleep because she pulled a late night on her thesis. This gives the game a completely natural, non-arbitrary, non-"the algorithm decided you're ready" reason for the player to suddenly be doing the bakery run alone, or talking to the doctor alone. It is not that Mira decided Alex has graduated to A2. It is that Mira has her own life and it is getting in the way, the way real roommates' lives do, and Alex has to cope. The intervention rule for this phase: Mira only steps in if the player explicitly asks for help (a context-sensitive "Hilfe" prompt) or if an interaction has been failing for a noticeably long time (the NPC has repeated themselves three times, say) — and even then her help is smaller, a single clarifying word rather than a full translation, and often delivered by text message rather than in person, because she's not even physically there.

**Phase 3 — Complete Independence (B1 to C1):** Mira's thesis gets handed in. She is around again, but the relationship has changed — she is not the training-wheels roommate anymore, she is just a friend, an actual peer, and the two of them talk about real things: her stress about her upcoming interpreting exams, Alex's stress about the restaurant, actual gossip about the neighbors, actual disagreements about actual things (whose turn it is to deal with the landlord, whether the heating bill split is fair). No more translating. If anything the relationship starts to invert a little.

**The graduation moment, written out properly:** Somewhere in the B1 stretch of the game — the design intends roughly Day 60, adjustable — Mira has to give an important practice presentation for her interpreting program and she is nervous about it, and she asks Alex, in German, completely normally, the way you'd ask a friend, "kannst du mir zuhören und sagen, ob es klar ist?" — can you listen and tell me if it's clear? And Alex does. And this is the whole point: it is the first time in the entire game where Mira needs something *from* Alex in German, rather than the other way around. It is a small, quiet, completely unglamorous scene — nobody makes a speech about how far Alex has come — and that restraint is exactly why it should land. The player should just suddenly notice, mid-scene, "oh — I'm not translating anything in my head anymore, I'm just listening to my friend."

## SECTION 4 — THE ANATOMY OF DAY 1, TOLD PROPERLY, NOTHING SKIPPED

This is Alex's very first morning in the flat in Connewitz. Alex flew in the day before, met Mira for about ten minutes, dropped the suitcase in the room, and went straight to sleep because of the jet lag. So Day 1, in game terms, starts here: waking up. CEFR target for the whole day: A0, meaning genuinely zero — the player should not be expected to know a single German word walking in. Every single word introduced today gets introduced through an object, a gesture, or Mira's bilingual narration, never through a menu or a flashcard popup.

I am going to write every micro-level as its own numbered beat, exactly the way you asked, frame by frame, nothing folded together, the way a kid tells a story.

**MORGEN (Morning)**

1. Alex's eyes open. The ceiling is not the ceiling of Alex's old room. It is taller, and there is a crack near the light fixture shaped a little bit like a river on a map. This is a wordless beat — no German yet, just the camera lingering on the unfamiliar ceiling for a second, so the player feels the disorientation before anything else happens.
2. A phone alarm is going off on the little table by the bed. The player has to reach over and tap it to turn it off. The alarm sound stops. Silence.
3. Alex sits up. The blanket is unfamiliar — a duvet, thicker and puffier than what Alex is used to, folded strangely at the foot of the bed from restless sleeping.
4. Through the wall, muffled, Mira's voice can be heard, and she is talking to herself, the way she does, half in German: "Kaffee, Kaffee, wo ist... ah, da ist er." The player does not need to understand this. It is atmosphere. It is also, quietly, the very first German the player has ever heard in the game, and it is delivered completely naturally, by someone who doesn't know she's being listened to.
5. Alex gets out of bed. Feet touch a wooden floor, cold.
6. The player is given control and a single glowing interaction point: the bedroom door.
7. Alex opens the bedroom door. It creaks — a small, specific, textured creak, the same creak every time, because it will become a recognizable little audio signature of this door for the rest of the game.
8. Mira, standing in the hallway with a mug in her hand, turns around, sees Alex, and her whole face lights up. She says, clearly, slowly, with a big warm smile: "Guten Morgen!" And then, immediately, in the same breath, translating for herself out loud the way she always does: "Guten Morgen — good morning!"
9. The player gets a simple response prompt with exactly one option the first time this ever happens (no wrong answers on Day 1, ever — this is a design rule for the whole day): tap to have Alex say "Guten Morgen" back. Mira beams.
10. Mira gestures at herself with her free hand and says "Ich bin Mira," and then, translating: "I'm Mira." She points at Alex, eyebrows raised, an obvious question gesture.
11. Player-prompted beat: Alex introduces themselves — "Ich bin Alex" — either typed, spoken (if voice input is enabled), or selected from a short list, again with the English translation shown alongside so there's zero ambiguity about what just happened.
12. Mira claps once, delighted, and says "Schön dich kennenzulernen!" then immediately: "Nice to meet you!"
13. Mira holds up her mug and raises her eyebrows — a clear "do you want one too?" gesture — and says "Kaffee?"
14. This is the very first micro-level with a real (still gentle) choice: yes or no. If yes, Mira says "Okay, komm mit!" (come with me) and gestures down the hallway toward the kitchen. If no, she shrugs, smiles, says "Okay, kein Problem!" and heads to the kitchen anyway, and Alex follows a beat later because there's nowhere else to go — the game gently makes sure the player ends up in the kitchen either way, since that's where the next dozen micro-levels live.
15. Walking down the hallway: a small interactive beat where the player can look at three framed photos on the wall (Mira as a child, Mira's parents, a Leipzig skyline print). These are optional, flavor-only, no words attached yet, just world-texture.
16. Entering the kitchen. New room, camera does a small establishing pan — table, two chairs, a window with a plant on the sill, a stovetop, a fridge, a coffee machine hissing.
17. Mira walks to the coffee machine, points at it, says "die Kaffeemaschine," taps it once with a finger for emphasis.
18. Mira pours a cup, hands it toward Alex (or, if Alex said no to coffee, sets it down for herself instead and gestures at the fridge next) — either way the next beat is the same.
19. Mira opens the fridge. Cold light spills out. She points inside, naming things quickly, one at a time, tapping each item as she says it: "Milch" (tapping a milk carton), "Butter" (tapping a butter dish), "Marmelade" (tapping a jam jar), "Eier" (tapping a carton of eggs).
20. The player gets a very soft, ungraded interaction here: tap any item in the fridge to hear Mira say its name again, as many times as wanted, no pressure, no timer, no score.
21. Mira closes the fridge, sets a few things on the counter — bread, the butter, the jam — and gestures at the table: "Setz dich!" then "Sit down!"
22. Alex sits. Mira sits across from her with her coffee.
23. Mira picks up the bread knife, holds it up, says "das Messer" — the knife — and then cuts a slice of bread, narrating "ich schneide das Brot" half to herself.
24. Mira slides the plate toward Alex and says "Brot?" — a simple offer.
25. Player choice, still zero-risk: yes or no to bread. If yes, Alex takes a slice; the animation shows Alex actually eating a bite, chewing, a small satisfied nod — small human warmth, not just a menu selection resolving.
26. Mira points at the butter and the jam in turn — "Butter," "Marmelade" — a light repeat of vocabulary from the fridge a minute ago, which is the game's very first tiny example of same-day spaced repetition: the words just got said twice, five minutes apart, in two different contexts (inside the fridge, on the table).
27. A small ambient beat: outside the kitchen window, a tram bell rings faintly in the distance. This is not tied to a word yet. It is planting a sound the player will hear meaningfully later — Akt II is going to be built around the tram.
28. Breakfast winds down. Mira stands, picks up both mugs and both plates, and says "Ich räum auf" — narrating herself again — then "I'm cleaning up."
29. Mira turns to Alex and says, more slowly and clearly than anything so far because this is a genuinely new, important line, "Heute gehen wir einkaufen, okay?" and immediately: "Today we're going shopping, okay?"
30. Alex nods (an auto-beat, not a choice — the story needs to move to the shop today regardless).

**VORMITTAG (Mid-morning) — Getting Ready**

31. Cut to Alex back in the bedroom, suitcase still half-unpacked on the floor. The player is given a light task: open the suitcase (single tap) and see clothes inside.
32. Mira pokes her head in the doorway and says, gesturing at her own outfit, "Es ist ein bisschen kalt heute" — pointing out the window — "cold today, ein bisschen kalt." She mimes shivering slightly, a clear, unmissable physical gesture attached to the word "kalt."
33. The player picks an outfit for Alex from a small wardrobe interaction (two or three simple choices — jacket A or jacket B, for instance). This is cosmetic/flavor, not graded, but the game plays a one-word confirmation either way, Mira glancing over and saying "gut" (good) with a thumbs up.
34. Alex gets dressed — a short, tasteful animation transition, not lingering.
35. Bathroom detour, introduced as a small optional side-room: the player can walk in, and there's a single soft interactive beat — Mira, brushing her own teeth in the doorway mirror at the same time (comic, ordinary, roommate-y), garbled through a mouth full of toothpaste, still manages to say "die Zahnbürste" while pointing at the toothbrush with her free hand. Alex can pick up their own toothbrush (from the suitcase, brought from home) and brush too — small animation, no words required from the player, just world-texture and the word "die Zahnbürste" logged softly for later, not tested today.
36. Back to the hallway. Coats by the door. Mira points at a coat hook and says "die Jacke," patting her own jacket.
37. Mira points at the door itself and says "die Tür," and then, hand on the handle, "Gehen wir?" — shall we go? — a friendly little check-in question rather than a command.
38. Player taps to confirm. Mira opens the door. Cold air, a burst of outside ambient sound (birds, a distant car, the tram bell again, closer this time).
39. Stepping out of the building. The player gets their first proper outdoor establishing shot of the street — Connewitz, a real, researched, lived-in-feeling street, cobblestones, a corner Späti (late-night convenience shop) that's actually open, bikes chained to a railing.
40. Mira points down the street and says "die Bäckerei ist da drüben" — the bakery's over there — and starts walking, and the player follows.

**MITTAG (Approaching Noon) — The Bäckerei**

41. Arriving outside the Bäckerei. A little bell above the door is visible through the glass, waiting to be triggered. The smell-implication is delivered visually — warm light, steam on the inside of the window glass, trays of visible bread shapes through the window.
42. Mira pushes the door open. The bell rings — a real, specific, recognizable bell sound, same every single time, because this bell is going to become one of the most repeated audio cues in the entire early game.
43. Inside: a warm, small, detailed bakery. A woman behind the counter — this is an important recurring NPC, she needs a name, she needs a face the player will recognize for the next hundred days of gameplay: her name is **Frau Nowak**, she's in her fifties, apron, flour dust on her forearms, glasses pushed up into her hair.
44. Frau Nowak looks up, smiles at Mira first (they clearly know each other) and says warmly "Hallo Mira!"
45. Mira says "Hallo Frau Nowak!" back, then gestures at Alex and says something quick to Frau Nowak in German that the game does not translate on-screen — a deliberate choice, this is the player's first taste of "adults are talking near me in German and I don't catch all of it and that's completely fine," which is realistic and is meant to feel normal, not scary.
46. Frau Nowak turns to Alex directly, still smiling, and says slowly, clearly, "Hallo! Was möchten Sie?" — what would you like? — using the formal "Sie" form, which is quietly, correctly modeled here because that is how a shopkeeper actually speaks to a stranger, and the game is going to keep this distinction (Sie vs. du) consistent and accurate throughout, without explaining the grammar term yet, just modeling correct usage.
47. Mira, standing just behind Alex's shoulder, murmurs the scaffolding line, soft and quick, not taking over, just feeding it: "sag einfach 'ein Brot, bitte'" — just say "a bread, please."
48. This is the very first micro-level in the entire game where the player produces a full German phrase on their own initiative, even if it was just fed to them a second ago. Player selects/speaks/types: "Ein Brot, bitte."
49. Frau Nowak's whole face does a warm, genuine, unforced little glow of pleasant surprise — not exaggerated, not a cartoon reaction, just a real small "oh, nice" — and she says "Sehr gut!" while reaching for a loaf and starting to bag it.
50. She names the bread as she picks it up — "ein Roggenbrot" (a rye bread), holding it up so the player sees the actual visual object tied to the actual new word, before bagging it.
51. She says a price out loud, clearly, and the game shows the number both spoken and written on a small receipt prop: "Zwei Euro fünfzig, bitte." This is the day's very first exposure to numbers in context, deliberately taught through a real transaction rather than a counting drill.
52. Player interaction: a simple, physical "pay" gesture — select coins/notes from a small wallet UI that mimics actually handling German coins and notes, tap to hand them over. No punishment for fumbling; Frau Nowak just waits, patiently, the way real shopkeepers do for a confused-looking newcomer.
53. Frau Nowak hands over the change, counting it out loud slowly as she does — "eine... zwei... zwei fünfzig, stimmt genau" (that's exactly right) — reinforcing the numbers a second time within the same sixty seconds, another small immediate spaced-repetition moment.
54. She hands over the bagged bread. Alex takes it — a small tactile animation, the warmth of the bag implied by a tiny steam VFX puff.
55. Frau Nowak says "Danke schön, bis bald!" — thanks, see you soon — and Mira echoes "Tschüss!" on the way out, waving.
56. The bell rings again on the way out — same bell sound as before, and this repetition is intentional, the bell becomes a little Pavlovian marker for "you just completed a real transaction."

**NACHMITTAG (Afternoon) — The Supermarkt**

57. Cut to walking further down the street. Mira points ahead to a larger shop — a Supermarkt, bigger, brighter signage, automatic doors.
58. Automatic doors slide open with a soft whoosh as Alex approaches — a satisfying, game-y, slightly exaggerated sound cue marking the transition into a bigger, more complex space than the cozy bakery.
59. Inside: shopping baskets stacked by the entrance. Mira picks one up, hands it to Alex — "der Einkaufskorb," the shopping basket, said once, clearly, not dwelt on.
60. First aisle: produce. Mira walks Alex past a few bins, naming things quickly and pointing, the same rapid-fire naming pattern from the fridge this morning, deliberately mirrored so the player recognizes the teaching pattern repeating: "Äpfel" (apples), "Bananen" (bananas), "Tomaten" (tomatoes), "Kartoffeln" (potatoes).
61. Soft ungraded interaction again: the player can tap any produce bin to hear the word again, freely, no pressure.
62. Alex picks two items into the basket (player choice, cosmetic, flavor) — a light physical animation of grabbing and placing.
63. Next aisle: dairy and eggs. This deliberately echoes this morning's fridge vocabulary a third time today — milk, butter, eggs — same words, third context (fridge, breakfast table, now the shop shelf itself), which is the day's clearest, most deliberate spaced-repetition thread, and it should feel satisfying rather than repetitive, like the player is starting to actually recognize things.
64. Mira checks her phone, makes a small "oh" face, and says "Ich muss kurz telefonieren" — I need to make a quick call — and steps a few meters away, still visible, still in the same room, but not hovering right at Alex's shoulder for a minute. This is a tiny, tiny, very early seed of the Phase 2 withdrawal, happening here on Day 1 in the smallest possible dose, just so it doesn't feel like a sudden personality change sixty days later — Mira has always had her own life, even on Day 1, just in small doses.
65. During this short window, a checkout worker — a young guy, headphones around his neck, name badge reading **Jonas** — is stocking a nearby shelf and glances at Alex's basket, says, casually, not even really a big interaction, just texture: "Alles klar?" (everything okay / all good?) — a very common, very casual real-life phrase, introduced here purely as ambient overheard speech, not something the player is required to respond to.
66. Mira returns, tucking her phone away, and says "Fertig?" — done? — gesturing at the basket.
67. The two head to checkout. A short queue (one other NPC ahead, wordless, flavor only).
68. At the register: the cashier, an older man, scans items with a beep-beep-beep sound, and says the total, again clearly and with the number shown on screen, reinforcing numbers a second time today in a second real transactional context.
69. Player pays again — same simple coin/note interaction as the bakery, deliberately reused so the player recognizes "oh, I know how to do this now" rather than learning a whole new payment interface twice in one day.
70. Bags packed (a small optional mini-interaction — drag items into a bag, entirely cosmetic, very short, mostly there for tactile satisfaction).

**ABEND (Evening) — Coming Home**

71. Walking back. The light in the sky has shifted to late-afternoon gold — a visual cue, no words attached, just atmosphere marking time passing.
72. Back at the flat. The specific creaky door sound from this morning plays again on entry — same creak, recognized.
73. Mira sets bags down on the kitchen counter and says, stretching, "Puh, das war viel!" — phew, that was a lot! — a natural, slightly tired, end-of-errands line.
74. Small unpacking interaction — putting groceries away, tapping fridge/cupboard, brief and optional, mirrors this morning's fridge-opening beat in reverse.
75. Mira glances at the clock on the wall, says "Ich koche heute — magst du Nudeln?" (I'm cooking tonight — do you like pasta?) and this is where the game gently, warmly nods at Alex's actual profession without making a big speech about it: as Mira starts pulling out a pot, Alex — unprompted, a small automatic character beat, not a player choice — steps in and quietly takes over stirring or seasoning something, comfortable and capable in a kitchen in a way Alex has not been comfortable or capable at anything else all day. Mira notices, raises an eyebrow, impressed, says "Oh! Du kannst kochen?" (oh, you can cook?) with real warmth.
76. Small, wordless, very human beat: Alex smiles — the first real, easy, un-anxious smile of the day — while cooking. No German required from the player here. This beat exists purely so the player feels, physically, the contrast between "I am lost with language" and "I am not lost at everything," which matters enormously for keeping an adult player's dignity intact on a hard first day.
77. Dinner at the table. A soft, low-key wind-down conversation from Mira — mostly her talking, Alex nodding, one or two more zero-risk single-word response prompts recycled from earlier in the day (does Alex want more bread — "Brot?" — yes/no, an intentional callback to the bakery word from this morning).
78. Mira says, gently, near the end of the meal, "Morgen zeige ich dir mehr — the tram, die Straße, alles" (tomorrow I'll show you more — the tram, the street, everything), setting up tomorrow's content out loud, in-fiction, rather than through a menu screen.

**NACHT (Night)**

79. Alex, alone now, back in the bedroom. A short reflective beat — no German required — Alex takes out the small photograph of Tomás, looks at it for a moment under the lamp, sets it on the little bedside table, propped up. This is the day's only nod to the B-plot, kept extremely small and quiet on purpose, because Day 1 needs to be entirely about survival, not backstory.
80. Lights off. Screen fades to black. End of Day 1.

That's eighty beats, which sits comfortably inside the "40 to 100 micro-events" range the brief for the whole game specified, right in the upper-middle of it, appropriate for a Day 1 that's doing a lot of foundational teaching work.

## SECTION 5 — THE ANATOMY OF DAY 15, TOLD PROPERLY, NOTHING SKIPPED

Two weeks have passed in-fiction (the days between Day 1 and Day 15 are separately designed elsewhere in the full production plan — this bible covers the two anchor days specifically requested). By Day 15, Alex has done the bakery run alone a handful of times, has started at Katarina's restaurant, has ridden the tram at least once with Mira watching closely. CEFR target: late A1 tipping into A2. The whole design intent of this day, written out plainly: things should feel recognizably harder and busier than Day 1, but never impossible, and — critically — Mira needs to visibly be one notch less present than she was two weeks ago, without ever announcing "scaffolding reduced," because her thesis deadline is now genuinely closer and that is a real, in-fiction, believable reason for her to be slightly more absent today than she was on Day 1.

**MORGEN (Morning)**

1. Alarm goes off. Same sound as Day 1 — a deliberate callback, the player should register "oh, I know this sound" instantly, which is itself a small quiet signal of how far they've come.
2. Alex turns it off — same gesture, now slightly faster in the animation timing, a subtle pacing cue that the player (and Alex) are simply quicker at mornings now.
3. Through the wall: not Mira's voice this time, but silence, then the muffled, specific clack of a laptop keyboard — Mira is already up and already working on her thesis. This is the day's very first, wordless signal that something about the household rhythm has shifted.
4. Alex gets up, same cold-floor beat as Day 1, no dialogue needed, pure physical continuity.
5. Bedroom door — same creak.
6. Hallway. Mira is not standing there waiting this time. Instead there's a sticky note on the hallway mirror, handwritten, in German, short and simple enough for an A1/A2 player to actually read unaided: "Bin am Schreiben, Kaffee ist da! Bis später — M." (Writing away, coffee's ready! See you later — M.) This is a genuinely important design beat: the game is now delivering scaffolding through *reading* a short authentic note instead of through Mira speaking aloud in person, which is a real, natural way reading skills get folded in without ever becoming a worksheet.
7. Player taps the note to have it read aloud once, optionally, for support — but critically, unlike Day 1, this is opt-in rather than automatic, the words don't just get said at the player, the player has to choose to seek the help, a small structural way of putting the player more in the driver's seat.
8. Kitchen. Coffee machine already has a full pot sitting there, warm, evidence of Mira having made it earlier and left. A single mug is set out with a small drawn smiley face on the sticky note leading into the kitchen — a nice bit of characterful staging that needs zero German at all, just tells the story visually.
9. Alex pours their own coffee — same coffee machine, same object, same word from two weeks ago ("die Kaffeemaschine") now used with total physical ease, no prompt needed at all this time, testing the word's retention purely through unprompted correct use.
10. Fridge — opened solo this time. The same items as Day 1 are still there — Milch, Butter, Marmelade, Eier — plus one new item that's appeared over two weeks of shopping trips: a jar of Nutella-brand hazelnut spread with a small hand-drawn heart on a sticky note from Mira reading "NICHT ANFASSEN 😄" (do not touch, with a laughing emoji) — a warm, funny, completely believable roommate joke, and also a nice bit of incidental reading practice around a mock-stern command form.
11. Alex makes their own breakfast alone — bread, butter, jam — same objects as Day 1, no dialogue prompts needed for any of it, a full solo repeat of a scene that needed heavy support two weeks ago.
12. A phone buzzes on the counter. It's a text from Mira: "Wie geht's? Alles gut?" (How's it going? All good?) — a short, real, A1-appropriate text message.
13. Player selects a short reply from a few natural options ("Alles gut!", "Ja, super!", "Ein bisschen müde" — a little tired) — the game's first fully text-based, no-audio-support production moment, deliberately different in format from every spoken interaction so far.
14. Mira replies with a single thumbs-up emoji and "Muss weiterschreiben, sorry!" (Have to keep writing, sorry!) — reinforcing, gently, in-fiction, exactly why she's not around this morning.

**VORMITTAG (Mid-morning) — Solo Errands Begin**

15. Alex checks a small paper list left on the counter (another authentic-reading beat) — today's tasks, handwritten by past-Alex the night before, in simple German with a few English words mixed in the way an actual beginner's own notes would look: "1. Post Office - Brief schicken (send a letter). 2. Neue Straßenbahnkarte kaufen (buy new tram ticket). 3. Apotheke - Kopfschmerztabletten (headache tablets, if there's time)."
16. This list is doing real structural work: it's the day's whole spine, replacing "Mira tells you what's happening next" from Day 1 with "you already know your own plan," a clean, diegetic marker of independence.
17. Getting dressed — same wardrobe interaction as Day 1, but now the game offers a slightly wider set of options (weather-appropriate layering, since it's been established as a bit colder two weeks into the season), reflecting slightly increased complexity everywhere, even in small systems.
18. Leaving the flat alone. Same door, same creak, but this time nobody says "Gehen wir?" — Alex just goes, unprompted, and the absence of that line is itself the point.
19. Onto the street. The Bäckerei is visible in the distance but today's list doesn't need it — a nice bit of restraint, not every day revisits every location, which matters for making the whole city feel lived-in and continuous rather than a checklist.
20. First stop: the tram stop (Straßenbahnhaltestelle), a new-ish location, used once before with Mira but never solo. A small digital display board shows the next tram's arrival countdown and line number — real informational text the player needs to actually read to know which tram to board.
21. A ticket machine stands at the stop. This is a genuinely new interaction type: a little in-world UI panel with real German button labels (Einzelfahrt — single ticket, Tageskarte — day ticket, and so on).
22. The player has to select the right ticket type based on something established on the paper list a few beats ago ("neue Straßenbahnkarte kaufen") — a small but real moment of applying information read in one place to a task completed in another, which is exactly the kind of joined-up real-world language use the whole game is chasing.
23. Coins go in — same core "handle German cash" interaction pattern from Day 1's bakery and supermarket, now its third outing, fully familiar, deliberately unremarkable to perform.
24. Ticket prints. A small satisfying physical animation — Alex takes it, checks it, tucks it away.
25. The tram itself arrives — a genuinely new, bigger sensory moment: doors hiss open, an internal announcement voice reads the next stop name aloud over a tinny speaker, other passengers are visible sitting and standing.
26. Alex boards. An elderly NPC passenger nearby, unprompted, says something short and polite, a real overheard-on-transit line: "Ist hier noch frei?" (Is this seat free?) gesturing at the seat beside Alex.
27. Player response: a simple "Ja, bitte" (yes, please) — a tiny, low-stakes, extremely realistic public-transit interaction, the first time the game has modeled "a total stranger just spoke to you out of nowhere and you have to respond in real time," which is a real and important skill milestone distinct from shopkeeper transactions.
28. The tram announcement calls out the stop Alex needs (matching something the player read on the display board earlier) — a listening-comprehension beat requiring the player to actually recognize the spoken stop name to know when to get off, not just watch a glowing marker.
29. Alex exits at the right stop. Small positive-feedback beat (not a big fanfare, just a clean, competent animation of stepping off) if correct; if the player mistimes it, the tram simply continues and Alex has to get off at the next stop and walk back — a real, low-punishment, realistic consequence rather than a hard fail state.

**MITTAG (Noon) — The Post Office**

30. Arriving at the Post Office (die Post) — a new interior location, brighter and more bureaucratic-feeling than the bakery, a small taste of the Bürokratie flavor that'll dominate Act III later, introduced here in a gentle, low-stakes early dose.
31. A ticket-number dispenser machine by the door — press a button, get a small printed number, a real and very German piece of everyday infrastructure.
32. A waiting area with a small screen cycling numbers. Ambient, wordless tension-free waiting beat.
33. Number called. A clerk at a counter — a new NPC, brisk but not unfriendly, name badge reads **Herr Baumann.**
34. Herr Baumann says, at a naturally faster pace than Frau Nowak used two weeks ago on Day 1 (a deliberate, carefully tuned difficulty increase in the audio itself, not just the vocabulary), "Guten Tag, was kann ich für Sie tun?" (Good day, what can I do for you?)
35. Player produces, from the day's paper list plus two weeks of accumulated vocabulary, a full small sentence rather than a single word this time: "Ich möchte einen Brief schicken, bitte" (I'd like to send a letter, please) — a clear, deliberate escalation from Day 1's single-word "Ein Brot, bitte."
36. Herr Baumann asks a genuine follow-up question requiring actual comprehension rather than just producing a memorized line: "Wohin? National oder international?" (Where to? Domestic or international?)
37. Player selects the correct answer based on context established earlier (the letter, established off-screen in backstory, is going to a cousin still in Mexico) — "International, nach Mexiko."
38. Herr Baumann weighs the letter, names a price, same numbers-in-context pattern established on Day 1, now a third or fourth repetition across two weeks, tested here in a higher-stakes, faster-paced setting.
39. Payment — same core cash-handling interaction, fourth or fifth outing by now, fully unremarkable to execute, precisely because unremarkable-and-automatic is the actual goal of spaced repetition.
40. Stamp gets applied with a satisfying thunk sound and visual. Letter goes into a slot. Small clean completion beat.

**NACHMITTAG (Afternoon) — The Unexpected Event)**

41. Leaving the post office, phone buzzes again. Not Mira this time — a text from **Chef Katarina** at the restaurant, introducing this important recurring character for the first time in an active, plot-relevant way rather than pure backstory: "Kannst du heute früher kommen? Wir haben ein Problem in der Küche." (Can you come in earlier today? We have a problem in the kitchen.)
42. This is Day 15's designated "unexpected event" beat, exactly the kind the original brief calls for ("missing keys, a broken washing machine, a neighbor's birthday, doctor's appointment") — here it's a minor workplace emergency, which also happens to double as the day's natural bridge into showing the restaurant as a real location for the first time.
43. Player reply, text-based again like this morning's exchange with Mira, deliberately mirroring that earlier beat's format: "Ok, bin unterwegs!" (Ok, on my way!) selected from short natural options.
44. Small plan-adjustment beat: the Apotheke (pharmacy) errand from this morning's list — item 3, explicitly marked "if there's time" — visibly does not get done today. The game does not punish this. It simply carries it over, unresolved, onto a future day's list, which is exactly how real errand-running actually works and reinforces that not everything needs to resolve same-day.
45. Quick walk to the restaurant, a new significant recurring location. Exterior first: modest signage, warm window light, a chalkboard menu outside with a few dish names in German the player can optionally read.
46. Inside: the kitchen, mid-chaos-but-controlled, exactly the kind of environment Alex is actually, genuinely good in, unlike almost everywhere else in the game so far — an important emotional rhyme with Day 1's dinner-cooking beat.
47. Chef Katarina, stressed but glad to see Alex, speaks fast, dropping in and out of a couple of English words the way someone genuinely bilingual and rushed actually does, modeling a realistic register the game hasn't used yet: "Alex! Gott sei Dank — the delivery, es ist falsch, alles falsch, kannst du mal schauen?" (Alex! Thank god — the delivery, it's wrong, all wrong, can you take a look?)
48. Player, now fully in their professional element, works through a short kitchen-specific vocabulary micro-sequence — checking delivery crates, naming ingredients that are present versus missing, using kitchen-register German (kitchens have their own register, tighter, more clipped, more command-form-heavy, and Act I is allowed one small authentic taste of that register here without teaching it formally yet).
49. Problem resolved (player taps through 3–4 short ingredient-check beats). Katarina, relieved, switches back to warmer, slower German: "Danke, Alex. Du rettest mich." (Thank you, Alex. You're saving me.)
50. A nice small quiet beat: this is the first time in the whole game someone has said Alex is good at something, in German, and Alex visibly understood it without needing translation — no fanfare, just a small proud half-smile animation.

**ABEND (Evening) — A Shift and a Message**

51. Time-skip forward through a short, mostly-visual dinner service montage — a handful of very short, optional, low-stakes order-taking micro-beats with restaurant customers (2–3 tiny transactional exchanges, deliberately brief since this isn't the day's main focus, just texture establishing the restaurant as a living location for future days).
52. Shift ends. Alex, tired in the good way, heads out the back door into the cool evening air.
53. Phone buzzes one more time — Mira again, finally surfacing properly for the first time all day: "Fertig mit dem Kapitel! 🎉 Bist du zu Hause?" (Finished with the chapter! Are you home?)
54. Player reply: "Noch nicht, war in der Arbeit. Bin bald da." (Not yet, was at work. Be there soon.) — a genuinely more complex, two-clause self-produced sentence than anything Day 1 asked for, a clear, deliberately visible marker of growth built right into the interaction itself rather than stated anywhere in a menu.
55. Walking home. The tram stop from this morning is passed again — same location, different time of day, lit up now against the dark, a nice visual rhyme.
56. Back at the flat. Same door, same creak — a callback for the third time today, now genuinely comforting rather than just familiar.
57. Mira is in the kitchen, looking pleasantly exhausted, a stack of printed pages beside her, and this time she greets Alex properly, in person, the way she did every single time on Day 1 — a deliberate structural choice, her Day 1 warmth returns fully now that her deadline pressure has lifted for the evening, so the "withdrawal" of the day doesn't read as a personality change, just a temporary, believable, resolved circumstance.
58. Mira asks, grinning, gesturing at Alex's slightly food-stained work shirt, "Wie war die Arbeit?" (How was work?) — how was work — using past tense for the first time directed at Alex all game, a small, carefully placed grammar seed (simple past-tense question form) landing naturally in conversation rather than as a drilled rule.
59. Player responds with a short summary sentence assembled from the day's actual events, selected from a few natural options: "Anstrengend, aber gut!" (Tiring, but good!)
60. The two sit down together for a low-key end-of-day meal — a clear structural echo of Day 1's dinner scene, same table, same two chairs, but the conversation now flows a little faster, a little more naturally, both characters interrupting each other slightly, laughing at a small joke about the Nutella jar from this morning, everything just slightly more alive than it was two weeks ago.

**NACHT (Night)**

61. Alone in the bedroom again. Same lamp. The photograph of Tomás is still propped on the little table where Alex left it two weeks ago, exactly where it's been sitting, quietly, this whole time.
62. Alex picks it up again — a small, wordless beat, mirroring Day 1's ending precisely — but this time, unlike Day 1, Alex sets it back down and, notably, does not look troubled or stuck the way Day 1's ending felt; there's the faintest suggestion, purely visual, of a plan starting to form, nothing stated outright, just a slightly longer, slightly more thoughtful look before the light goes off. This plants, very quietly, that the B-plot is going to start moving once the player has enough German to actually chase it — which is exactly what Act III is for.
63. Lights off. Fade to black. End of Day 15.

Sixty-three beats — comfortably inside the 40–100 range again, and noticeably more compact than Day 1's eighty, which is intentional: Day 15 spends less time on pure vocabulary introduction (because so much of today is recycled Day 1 vocabulary in new contexts) and more time on longer, more complex individual exchanges, which is exactly the kind of shift that should keep happening, day over day, all the way through the whole design.

## SECTION 6 — THE SPATIAL PROGRESSION MAP, EVERY ROOM, NOTHING SKIPPED

Same rule as before: every zone gets listed, every object inside that zone gets listed, and what that object teaches gets listed right next to it. No summarizing a room as "kitchen items" — every single item, one at a time.

### ACT I ZONES — Das Erwachen (A0 → A1)

**Zone: Alex's Bedroom**
- Bed / das Bett — first-exposure word, taught through waking-up beats
- Blanket/duvet / die Bettdecke — taught through the physical unfamiliar-texture beat on Day 1
- Window / das Fenster — used for weather-checking micro-levels across many days
- Wardrobe / der Kleiderschrank — the outfit-selection interaction lives here
- Bedside table / der Nachttisch — home of the Tomás photograph, a quiet emotional anchor object
- Suitcase / der Koffer — only interactive in the first few days, phased out once unpacking is "complete," a nice bit of environmental storytelling (a room that changes as the player settles in)
- Door / die Tür — the recurring creak-sound door, functions almost like a character due to sheer repetition

**Zone: Hallway**
- Mirror / der Spiegel — used for the Day 15 sticky-note beat, and later for simple self-description practice in Act II ("Ich bin müde," etc.)
- Coat hooks / die Garderobe — jacket selection lives here
- Framed photos (flavor only, no formal vocabulary target, pure world-texture)

**Zone: Kitchen**
- Coffee machine / die Kaffeemaschine — the single most-repeated object-word in the whole early game by design
- Fridge / der Kühlschrank — houses Milch, Butter, Marmelade, Eier, Nutella-with-sticky-note; the fridge is the primary spaced-repetition engine of Act I, since its contents get named, re-named, and referenced in three separate contexts within Day 1 alone
- Stovetop / der Herd — mostly flavor in Act I, becomes mechanically important later when Alex starts cooking at home, not just at the restaurant
- Kitchen table / der Tisch, chairs / die Stühle — the site of both anchor days' dinner scenes, a deliberately reused "home base" location for emotional beats
- Bread knife / das Messer — introduced via Mira slicing bread
- Sticky notes — not a "real" object exactly but a recurring interactive prop-type across the whole game, since they're the mechanism for asynchronous, readable (not spoken) scaffolding

**Zone: Bathroom**
- Toothbrush / die Zahnbürste
- Mirror / der Spiegel (a second instance of this word, deliberately, tying to the hallway mirror so the player notices the same word recurring in two rooms)
- Shower / die Dusche — flavor-only in Act I, gains a proper micro-level in later days not covered in this document's two anchor days

**Zone: The Street (Connewitz)**
- Corner Späti (late-night shop) — establishing-shot only in the two anchor days, becomes a full interactive location on other days in the broader 100-day plan
- Bike railings — flavor
- Cobblestones — pure atmosphere, no vocabulary target, exists for visual authenticity

**Zone: Die Bäckerei**
- Bell above door — an audio-signature object rather than a vocabulary object; its entire job is Pavlovian pacing, not teaching a word
- Bread display / die Brotauswahl — the visual array behind Frau Nowak, houses several bread-type words across multiple visits (Roggenbrot on Day 1; others seeded for future days)
- Till/register — houses the numbers-and-money teaching loop
- Frau Nowak herself — functionally an interactive "object" in game terms, a recurring NPC with a stable, memorable face and a stable formal-register speech pattern (always Sie, never du, correctly and consistently)

**Zone: Der Supermarkt**
- Automatic doors — audio/visual signature object, same pacing function as the bakery bell
- Shopping basket / der Einkaufskorb
- Produce bins — Äpfel, Bananen, Tomaten, Kartoffeln
- Dairy shelf — second-context repetition of Milch, Butter, Eier from the home fridge
- Checkout / die Kasse — second instance of the money-handling interaction pattern, deliberately reused rather than reinvented

### ACT II ZONES — Mein Kiez (A1 → A2)

**Zone: Straßenbahnhaltestelle (Tram Stop)**
- Digital arrival board — a pure-reading-comprehension object, no audio attached, deliberately, since reading a departures board unaided is a real skill worth isolating
- Ticket machine — houses real button-label vocabulary (Einzelfahrt, Tageskarte) and a proper in-world "choose the right option based on information gathered elsewhere" puzzle
- The tram itself — houses the internal announcement voice (a pure listening-comprehension object) and the stranger-on-transit interaction pattern ("Ist hier noch frei?")

**Zone: Café**
- Not deeply covered in the two anchor days above, but seeded for the wider Act II plan — houses ordering vocabulary one register more casual than the bakery's formal Sie-register, since cafés (per the design brief) are where du starts creeping in more naturally among younger patrons

**Zone: Die Post (Post Office)**
- Ticket-number dispenser — a small bureaucratic-ritual object, planting the seed of Act III's much heavier bureaucracy content in a gentle, low-stakes early dose
- Counter / der Schalter, with Herr Baumann as the recurring formal-register NPC for this location
- Letter, stamp, scale — the full send-a-letter transactional chain, each with its own small interactive beat

**Zone: Doctor's Clinic**
- Seeded in the wider Act II plan (symptom-describing vocabulary, per the original brief's Core Mastery list) though not covered in the two anchor days specifically requested

**Zone: Park**
- Seeded for small-talk-with-neighbors content per the original brief, seasonal/weather vocabulary reinforcement

### ACT III ZONES — Die Stadt & Die Ämter (A2 → B1)

**Zone: Chef Katarina's Restaurant**
- Delivery crates — the ingredient-check kitchen-register micro-sequence lives here
- The pass / die Ausgabe (where finished dishes are set for service) — a natural home for command-form and urgency-register German
- Front-of-house dining tables — short customer-order exchanges, functioning as a lighter, repeatable, low-stakes transactional loop the player can return to on many different days
- Katarina herself — the game's primary "bilingual code-switcher" NPC besides Mira, modeling a different, faster, more mixed-register speech pattern

**Zone: Bürgeramt (Citizens' Registration Office)**
- Seeded per the original brief's Act III content — Anmeldung (residence registration) paperwork, appointment-ticket systems, formal-register waiting-room interactions; not covered in the two anchor days but structurally a heavier, multi-step version of the Die Post pattern already established, so players arrive at it having already learned the "take a number, wait, speak to a formal clerk" ritual once before

**Zone: Alex's Workplace-Adjacent Social Web**
- Friends' apartments, per the original brief — seeded for roommate-dispute and social-negotiation content in the wider plan

### ACT IV ZONES — Das Wahre Leben (B1 → B2/C1)

**Zone: Law Office / Cultural Centers / Conference Rooms**
- Seeded per the original brief for high-register, high-stakes Konjunktiv II and professional-register content; this is also, per the narrative arc, where the Tomás B-plot is intended to finally become mechanically active — archive offices, old records counters, and a possible final emotional payoff location are earmarked here in the wider production plan, deliberately kept vague in this document since it falls outside the two anchor days requested and deserves its own dedicated pass once Acts III and IV are scoped in full.

## SECTION 7 — THE MASTER CURRICULUM MATRIX

This table is the systematic, at-a-glance version of everything told as a story above. Day 1 and Day 15 rows are fully detailed since those are the two days this document was asked to build in full; the other rows are directional placeholders showing how the same pattern is meant to continue across the whole 100-plus-day arc, so a production team has a skeleton to fill in when they scope the remaining days.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Day 1 — Act I | A0 | Waking up in the flat; meeting Mira; first bakery and supermarket trips | Greetings (Guten Morgen, Hallo); sein-conjugated self-introduction (Ich bin...); core household nouns (Kaffeemaschine, Kühlschrank, Brot, Messer); numbers 1–10 in a payment context; formal Sie register modeled by Frau Nowak | 80 |
| Day 2–4 — Act I | A0 → A1 | Settling-in days: learning the flat fully, first solo micro-errand attempts with Mira nearby but not hovering | der/die/das article drilling through repeated object-naming; basic verbs haben, wohnen; color words via clothing and household objects | ~50–60 per day (planned, not detailed in this document) |
| Day 5–9 — Act I | A1 | First trip to a café; first "Was ist das?" spontaneous-question milestone; Alex starts at the restaurant part-time | Question formation (Was ist das? Wo ist...?); food-ordering vocabulary; restaurant-register kitchen vocabulary begins seeding early | ~50–60 per day (planned) |
| Day 10–14 — Act I → Act II | A1 | Transition week: first solo tram ride with Mira watching from a distance; first Post Office visit together | Modal verb seeding (möchten introduced softly before Act II formalizes können/müssen); directional basics (hier, da, dort) | ~50–70 per day (planned) |
| Day 15 — Act II | A1 → A2 (transition) | Solo errand day: post office letter, tram ticket alone, unexpected restaurant emergency, Mira's thesis-driven partial withdrawal begins | Modal verbs in practice (möchte, kann); full-sentence self-production (Ich möchte einen Brief schicken); past-tense question form directed at player (Wie war die Arbeit?); stranger-on-transit small talk; text-message register as distinct from spoken register | 63 |
| Day 16–25 — Act II | A2 | Doctor's clinic visit (minor, low-stakes); park small talk with a recurring neighbor NPC; first genuine miscommunication-and-repair scene | Modal verbs müssen/können fully active; Perfekt (past tense) introduced properly through describing yesterday's events; symptom-description vocabulary; Akkusativ prepositions in direction-giving | ~60–80 per day (planned) |
| Day 26–40 — Act II → Act III | A2 → B1 (approach) | Roommate-logistics subplot (a shared bill dispute with Mira, resolved entirely in German, the first real interpersonal disagreement in the language); job responsibilities deepen at the restaurant | Dativ prepositions; comparative forms (teurer, billiger) for the bill dispute; early Nebensatz seeding via weil in casual speech before it's formalized | ~60–80 per day (planned) |
| Day 41–55 — Act III | B1 (approach) | Bürgeramt Anmeldung (residence registration) — the first major bureaucratic set-piece; a formal rental-contract (Mietvertrag) reading task | Nebensätze fully formalized (weil, dass, wenn, obwohl); formal written German comprehension (reading a real-feeling contract clause by clause); passive voice introduced through official signage and forms | ~70–90 per day (planned) |
| Day 56–65 — Act III | B1 | Job interview subplot (either a promotion at Katarina's restaurant or an external interview, tunable); Mira's graduation moment (Day ~60) | Präteritum (narrative past, distinct from conversational Perfekt); opinion-expression structures (ich finde, dass...; meiner Meinung nach); the full Mira role-reversal scene lands here, mechanically requiring sustained listening comprehension with zero translation support for the first time | ~70–90 per day (planned) |
| Day 66–80 — Act III → Act IV | B1 → B2 (approach) | The Tomás B-plot formally activates — Alex has enough German to start asking real questions about the great-uncle; first archive/records-office visit | Formal written-request German (Anträge, requesting official records); indirect speech (reported speech: "Sie sagte, dass..."); higher-register vocabulary tied to family history and identity | ~70–90 per day (planned) |
| Day 81–100+ — Act IV | B2 → C1 | High-stakes professional conflict (defending a project or handling a contract dispute per the original brief); resolution (or deliberate non-resolution, tunable) of the Tomás thread; a final, non-repetitive, fully independent "ordinary day" that mirrors Day 1's structure beat-for-beat as a closing callback, showing the same city through fluent eyes | Konjunktiv II fully active (hypotheticals, polite hedging, "Wenn ich das gewusst hätte..."); idiomatic Redewendungen; register-switching fluency (the player should be able to consciously choose formal vs. casual German depending on who they're speaking to, rather than the game choosing for them) | ~80–100 per day (planned) |

A structural note on the matrix, told plainly rather than left implicit in the table: the micro-level count is not meant to just keep climbing forever. It climbs through Act I and into Act II because there's a lot of brand-new foundational vocabulary to seed. But starting around Act III, the count staying roughly flat or even dipping slightly on some days is intentional — by B1, a "micro-level" is doing more grammatical and social work per beat than an A0 micro-level was, so the same rough number of beats per day is actually carrying a lot more linguistic weight than it did on Day 1. More beats is not automatically better design once the player is past the absolute-beginner stage; the right comparison is not "Day 90 should have more micro-levels than Day 1," it's "each micro-level on Day 90 should be doing noticeably more than each micro-level on Day 1 was able to do."
