# Ein Neues Leben in Deutschland

## Master Narrative Bible & Pedagogical Progression Architecture

**Format:** 3D life-simulation narrative RPG for German acquisition  
**Learning journey:** A0 entry point to operational C1  
**Campaign frame:** 360 named Story Days across one in-world year; 40–60 micro-events per Story Day; approximately 18,000 authored micro-events before optional replay variants.  
**Pedagogical reference:** CEFR-informed progression calibrated to the functional expectations of Goethe and telc examinations, without turning the world into an exam-prep menu.

### Creative promise

The player does not arrive in Germany as a student who has been assigned exercises. They arrive as a person with a key, a suitcase, an incomplete address, a tired body, and a reason to begin again. German is not a subject placed in front of them. It is the sound on the intercom, the label on the rubbish bin, the warm question from the baker, the form that must be understood before the office closes, and later the language in which they comfort a friend, defend a proposal, disagree with a landlord, and tell a joke that actually lands.

Every useful phrase has a physical reason to exist. Every repetition changes its social temperature. “Guten Morgen” begins as a survival line said to a neighbour; it becomes an automatic greeting, then a way of signalling concern when that neighbour looks ill. “Ich hätte gern …” begins at a bakery and later becomes a deliberately polite request in a meeting. The game never pretends that a single correct click makes a person fluent. Comprehension, recognition, retrieval, spoken production, repair after failure, and later nuance are all separate achievements.

## 1. Campaign architecture and pedagogical contract

### The 360-Day model

The story covers one calendar year in **Neustadt am Fluss**, a fictional, regionally neutral German city with recognisable national systems and no implied “one true Germany.” A Story Day is a curated narrative day. It contains 40–60 short, embodied events, but it is not a claim that twenty minutes of play equals twenty minutes of learning. Dialogue expands or contracts according to the player’s mode: guided listening, supported production, or independent production. Optional “ordinary days” reuse routines with altered weather, people, prices, and problems; they provide extra exposure without advancing plot too quickly.

The 360-day design is intentional. A credible A0→C1 path requires long exposure, repeated meaningful use, rest, and a growing life—not fifteen magical lessons. Day 15 is therefore **not** an A1→A2 transition. It is an early-A1 confidence threshold and a first bridge toward the kinds of independence demanded later in A1/A2. The player can greet, identify, ask for a few needs, and recover from a tiny misunderstanding; they cannot yet manage a clinic, tenancy law, or a fast debate.

| Act | Story days | CEFR movement | Narrative territory | Core emotional question |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| I. **Das Erwachen** | 1–90 | A0 → secure A1 | apartment, street, baker, supermarket | Can I make this unfamiliar place feel survivable? |
| II. **Mein Kiez** | 91–180 | A1 → secure A2 | neighbourhood, transit, café, post, clinic, park | Can I participate rather than merely get by? |
| III. **Die Stadt & Die Ämter** | 181–270 | A2 → secure B1 | city, Bürgeramt, work/study, travel, shared homes | Can I take responsibility for my own life here? |
| IV. **Das wahre Leben** | 271–360 | B1 → B2/C1 performance | culture, law, public debate, professional life | Can I belong without becoming less myself? |

### Micro-event design rule

Each micro-event has five layers, even when only one is visible to the player:

1. **Physical action:** turn a tap, choose a ticket, open a letter, carry a plate.
2. **Comprehensible cue:** object, gesture, sound, facial expression, sign, or context makes meaning guessable before translation.
3. **Language demand:** notice, understand, select, say, write, or repair a German construct.
4. **Human consequence:** comfort, embarrassment, delay, relief, affection, inconvenience, or new access—not arbitrary points.
5. **Return path:** the same language returns later in a new setting at greater speed, with less assistance, or a different pragmatic purpose.

Error is a scene, never a trap. Ordering the wrong roll may mean the player receives a sweet **Rosinenbrötchen** instead of a savoury roll. The baker smiles, asks a clarifying question, and the player has a memorable reason to say, “Nein, ohne Rosinen, bitte.” A safety-critical mistake never becomes humiliation; a legal or medical mistake always opens a repair path, a plain-language explanation, and a chance to try again.

### Natural repetition schedule

New language is recycled on a deliberately varied rhythm: immediate echo within the same interaction; a second encounter that day in a different sensory context; next-morning recall; 3-day social reuse; 7-day task reuse; 14-day mixed retrieval; 30-day independent use; then periodic high-pressure or emotionally meaningful reuse. The world records *function*, not only vocabulary. A player who can recognise **müssen** in a poster but freezes at “Sie müssen hier umsteigen” receives travel scenes that rehearse exactly that listening burden.

Daily routines anchor high-frequency language: pronouns, articles, numbers, clock time, separable verbs, food, hygiene, weather, household items, and polite formulas. Plot disruptions create desirable difficulty: a dark stairwell forces **Taschenlampe**, a missing parcel forces **abholen**, a rainy picnic forces **weil**, a rude email later forces register and boundary-setting. NPCs remember social choices, not merely grammar accuracy.

## 2. World premise and protagonist narrative arc

### Player character: the open but authored protagonist

The protagonist is customisable in name, appearance, gender expression, first language, profession/previous study, and precise reason for moving. The authored spine remains stable so the story has emotional weight: they have chosen a one-year restart in Neustadt am Fluss after a life that had become too narrow. A scholarship, a new job, family reconnection, a creative project, or a career change may be selected as the surface reason. Beneath it is the same quieter wound: at home, the protagonist had begun to speak from habit rather than possibility. They want to become someone who can act before they know every word.

The player arrives with a three-month sublet in **Lindenhof 27**, a temporary contract or course placement, a recommendation from a distant contact, and an address written by hand: “Mara Vogel, 3. Stock.” The phone battery is nearly empty. The immigration/registration process is real but humane; German bureaucracy is presented as a system with rules, limits, and people inside it—not a cartoon villain. The story treats identity, migration, money, health, and belonging with care. It never requires the player to disclose trauma or simulate immigration status they do not share.

### Arc in four movements

**Act I — The body arrives before the language.** The first enemy is not German grammar; it is cognitive overload. The protagonist does not know whether the blue bin is paper, whether the neighbour is angry, how to buy breakfast, or how to make the flat feel like theirs. Small successes are huge: finding the right key, saying a name clearly enough to be understood, buying bread without switching languages. Their first goal is concrete—complete the household list Mara left and send an “I arrived” message—but psychologically it is permission to remain.

**Act II — The self becomes visible.** The protagonist begins having recurring routes and recurring people: Mr. Özdemir at the kiosk, Mina at the café, Dr. Weber’s receptionist, children in the park, tram driver Anja. Familiarity makes language less staged. A late train, a postcard for home, a cold, and an invitation to a neighbourhood picnic teach the protagonist that asking for help is not failure. They form a small Kiez circle and learn to say more than needs: preferences, excuses, plans, memories, and gentle disagreement.

**Act III — A person takes responsibility.** Registration, a rental question, a job interview or university presentation, and conflict with a roommate force precise language. The protagonist cannot wait for someone to rescue them. They learn the difference between *being understood* and making a defensible case. A setback—a rejected application, an administrative delay, or an avoidable misunderstanding with Mara—tempts them to retreat into isolation. Their growth is to name the problem, ask questions, negotiate, and repair a relationship without surrendering dignity.

**Act IV — Belonging has a voice.** The player chooses a capstone civic/professional project: a neighbourhood cultural evening, accessible local guide, community garden initiative, exhibition, workplace proposal, or research event. A funding/venue dispute and a public discussion force them to handle irony, disagreement, incomplete evidence, emotionally charged language, and competing values. The climax is not “speaking perfect German.” It is choosing, in German, what kind of neighbour and colleague to be. In the final scene, they welcome a new arrival to Lindenhof 27. They give clear help, then wait long enough for the newcomer to answer for themself.

### Recurring cast

| **Samira Haddad** | café manager and community organiser; spoken speed, idiom, social register | becomes the player’s model for generous, direct belonging |
| **Herr Özdemir** | kiosk owner; numbers, small talk, local news | moves from transaction to trustworthy everyday witness |

| **Dr. Weber / Frau Seidel** | clinic doctor/receptionist | models clear consent-based health communication and formal register |

## 3. Shadow Guide dossier: Elian “Eli” Hartmann

### Identity and visual presence

**Name:** Elian Hartmann; they invite the player to call them **Eli**.  
**Age:** 31.  
**Role:** neighbour across the landing, city orientation volunteer, then friend and peer.  
**Background:** born in Neustadt to a German father and a Chilean mother; spent adolescence moving between cities and grew up translating not only words but moods. Eli studied social pedagogy, briefly worked in a welcome centre, and left when they realised that well-meant help can make people dependent. They now coordinate a small community library project three days a week and write short audio walks about overlooked corners of the city.

Eli is not a hovering hologram or a magical translator. They are a real person with a keyring, a bicycle with a squeaky rear brake, a messy herb garden, and evenings when they are unavailable. In early play they are physically near because Mara has asked them to check on the new subtenant. Later their presence becomes a phone call, a voice note, a chance meeting, or simply a remembered phrase. Their decreasing availability is not a mechanical removal; it is a relationship maturing.

### Personality, wound, and desire

Eli observes before speaking. Their humour is gentle and slightly sideways: “German compound nouns are just words refusing to move out.” They are patient with effort but allergic to performative helplessness. They never say “That’s easy,” because they remember adults using that phrase when a new country felt impossible. Their own flaw is over-responsibility. Eli has confused being useful with being close, and must learn that stepping back can also be care.

They want the player to have an independent life in their city—even if that means the player eventually needs them less. This creates a tender minor conflict in Act II: after Eli repeatedly lets the player answer first, the player misreads the distance as rejection. Eli explains, in simple German suited to the moment, “Ich gehe nicht weg. Ich gehe nur einen Schritt zurück.” By Act IV, Eli asks the player for help with a difficult public-library proposal, reversing the early rescue dynamic.

### Voice and performance direction

At A0, Eli speaks in short breath groups, with a pause after the key word and a matching gesture: “Das ist **der Schlüssel**. Der Schlüssel. Für die Tür.” They maintain natural intonation, not robotic baby talk. They use German first, then contextual support; translation is a player-controlled safety layer, never a substitute for listening. They repeat only the piece that matters and ask the player to do something with it: point, choose, bring, say, or write.

At A1–A2, Eli stops pre-translating routine speech. They use reformulation: player says “Ich brauche Post”; Eli answers, “Ah—du **möchtest einen Brief abschicken**.” At B1, Eli speaks at authentic speed but checks meaning in the flow: “Meinst du, dass…?” At B2/C1, they are neither teacher nor evaluator. They can disagree, use irony, be tired, and occasionally misunderstand; the player must navigate an equal relationship.

### Intervention ladder

| Stage | Intervention | Appropriate use | What Eli never does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Notice** | gaze, point, waits three seconds | object-word mapping; low stakes | supplies an answer before the player tries |
| 2. **Context cue** | gesture, visual focus, repeat one key word slowly | A0 misunderstanding; crowded sensory scenes | translates an entire conversation by default |
| 3. **Choice scaffold** | offers two meaningful German options | first encounters with food, directions, greetings | labels one option “correct” without context |
| 4. **Recast** | responds naturally with the more accurate phrase | player can communicate but form is developing | interrupts emotional speech for minor grammar |
| 5. **Repair prompt** | “Noch einmal?” / “Was meinst du genau?” | player request, ambiguity, social recovery | speaks over the player to an NPC |
| 6. **Safety intervention** | steps in, then gives control back | medical consent, legal deadlines, harassment, immediate danger | makes irreversible choices or hides the issue |

**Phase 1: complete shadowing (Days 1–35).** Eli supplies a German-first lifeline, stands beside the player, and makes objects meaningful. They intervene at the first sign of overload, but ask for a small return: name the object, repeat the greeting, point to the price, or choose the polite formula.

**Phase 2: scaffolding withdrawal (Days 36–180).** Eli arrives late, waits outside, or deliberately gives the player the first conversational turn. Help uses a three-part consent rule: wait; ask “Möchtest du einen Hinweis?”; offer the smallest hint that restores agency. If the player fails, Eli helps them repair it aloud instead of erasing the social consequence.

**Phase 3: peerhood (Days 181–360).** Eli cannot be summoned for ordinary tasks. The player may still call during designated emotional or safety scenes, but Eli asks questions rather than supplying language. They have their own needs, disagreements, and blind spots. Their friendship is strongest when the player’s German is least dependent on them.

### Graduation scene: “Die offene Tür” (Day 270)

After the player completes the Bürgeramt appointment, resolves a contractual misunderstanding, and sends a formal follow-up alone, Eli meets them in the Lindenhof stairwell. The hallway is ordinary: recycling smells faintly of cardboard, someone’s bicycle light blinks, rain taps the frosted window. Eli offers the spare emergency key given on Day 1. The player expects a ceremonial speech. Instead Eli says, “Du hast sie nicht mehr nötig.” The player may answer, “Doch. Als Schlüssel schon nicht. Als Freund*in schon.”

Eli smiles, gives the key back only if the player chooses to keep it, and hands over a blank library card—not a reward badge. “Jetzt zeigst du mir deine Stadt.” From that point they no longer provide language overlays. Their final tutorial action is not help but trust. The actual emotional graduation is completed on Day 360, when the protagonist—not Eli—helps the next newcomer pause, breathe, and speak.

## 4. Anatomy of Story Day 1 — “Der Schlüssel in der Hand”

**Pedagogical status:** A0, no assumed German.  
**Day promise:** The player can survive their first morning: recognise essential apartment nouns, greet one person, state their name, ask for water, buy a simple breakfast, and return home.  
**Language seed:** *Hallo, Guten Morgen, ich, du, sein, heißen, wohnen, der/die/das, hier, bitte, danke, ja/nein, Zahlen 0–10; Wasser, Tür, Schlüssel, Zimmer, Bett, Lampe, Bad, Küche, Brot.*  
**Emotional curve:** disorientation → small control → first public risk → quiet pride.

The player’s suitcase is still unopened. The flat is not a puzzle box; it is a living place whose labels, sounds, and slight untidiness carry meaning. Each event can be replayed with fewer visual labels. “Micro-level” means a bounded learning beat, not an artificial mini-game.

### Morgen — arrival and first orientation

1. **07:02, the alarm and bed.** A soft phone says “Guten Morgen.” The player stops it, touches the unfamiliar **Bett**, and hears Eli repeat the greeting through the wall. Goal: understand a morning sound before being asked to speak.
2. **Floor, curtain, window.** Cold feet make the player look for slippers; opening the curtain reveals Lindenstraße. Eli gives only “der Boden” and “das Fenster ist offen,” allowing **Bett/Boden** and **Fenster/offen** to be tied to touch and light.
3. **Room, lamp, suitcase.** A card says “Zimmer”; the player switches on **die Lampe**, opens **den Koffer**, and finds a jumper. “Dein Zimmer” is heard as welcome, not as a possessive-grammar lecture.
4. **Welcome note and key.** Mara’s note says “Willkommen! Küche, Bad, WLAN: morgen.” Eli reads *Willkommen* and lets the heavy **Schlüssel** clink in the player’s palm. The key is deliberately planted as the object that will later mean trust.
5. **Name exchange.** Eli points to themself: “Ich heiße Eli. Und du?” The player says/selects “Ich heiße …”; their name earns eye contact and a real reply, not a success animation.

### Morgen — bathroom and bodily needs

6. **Door, hallway, shoes.** The player tries the key in the bedroom **Tür**, steps into a hallway full of coats and **Schuhe**, and learns the door word from a practical mistake rather than a label.
7. **Find the bathroom.** A blue “Bad” card, then another **Lampe**, guides the player into the right room. The repeated light word is a first retrieval, not a new lesson.
8. **Mirror and location.** In the mirror Eli says, “Du bist hier.” The player sees their own face and absorbs **du/hier** as a reassuring fact.
9. **Tap, water, glass.** A dry throat supplies urgency. Eli says “das Wasser”; the player turns the tap, notices a clean glass, and asks or indicates “Wasser, bitte?” **bitte** has an immediate purpose.
10. **Brush, cap, towel.** The player chooses their unopened **Zahnbürste**, opens and closes toothpaste (*auf/zu*), and reaches for a towel after “Was brauchst du?” Eli supplies “Ich brauche …” only as a model for later.

### Vormittag — kitchen, breakfast, and first household relationship

11. **Kitchen arrival.** Coffee smell and refrigerator hum announce **die Küche**. Mara looks up: “Guten Morgen.” Returning the greeting earns a warm “Guten Morgen, [name].”
12. **Mara’s name.** Eli says, “Das ist [name].” Mara answers, “Ich bin Mara.” The player hears *ich heiße* and *ich bin* side by side, with two real people attached.
13. **Tee oder Kaffee?** Mara offers two mugs. Player points or answers **ja/nein**; if coffee is unwanted, Mara’s “Ah, kein Kaffee. Tee?” models a consequence-friendly repair.
14. **Fridge inventory.** The **Kühlschrank** contains **Milch**, butter, cheese, apple, and **Brot**. Eli asks “Was ist das?” only for bread, because a single successful answer matters more than exhaustive naming.
15. **Safety and setting.** Mara says “Vorsicht” while taking the knife; the player puts **Milch**, **Brot**, and a **Teller** on the counter. Articles arrive as sound patterns with objects, never as coloured exam labels.
16. **Two slices.** The bread bag shows “2.” Eli counts “eins, zwei”; the player puts two slices on the plate. Number becomes quantity rather than recital.
17. **Need a cup.** There is no mug. Eli waits long enough for gesture or speech, then recasts “Ich brauche eine Tasse.” Mara gives one; a need has been met through language.
18. **Danke / bitte.** Player says **Danke**; Mara replies **Bitte**. The reversal is experienced in conversation and will be heard again at the bakery.
19. **Taste and comfort.** Eli asks “Gut?” with a thumb gesture. The player says **gut/ja**, eats toast, and is permitted a small moment of ordinary safety.

### Vormittag — leaving the flat

20. **Mara’s list.** A picture-supported list reads **Wasser, Brot, Äpfel**. The player recognises two old words and meets plural *Äpfel* as a useful shopping image, not a declension problem.
21. **Ten euros.** Eli matches a €10 note to “zehn Euro,” explaining only enough money for today’s trip. The note is tangible rehearsal for the till.
22. **Jacket, shoes, rain.** Rain on the window and a radio forecast make **Regen** meaningful; player chooses **die Jacke** and **die Schuhe**. The old singular *Schuh* quietly becomes plural in use.
23. **Door ritual.** The player locks **die Tür**, checks **den Schlüssel** when Eli asks one calm “Schlüssel?”, and repeats “Hallo” once before meeting anyone.
24. **Frau Bender and address.** A neighbour says “Hallo” on the stairs. Player answers; outside the sign **Lindenstraße 27** gives a readable route home. Silence remains safe, but the later gentle recap offers the greeting again.

### Mittag — bakery as first public transaction

25. **Bakery window and queue.** Warm pretzel smell, bread labels, a bell, and two waiting adults make **die Bäckerei** legible. Player recognises **Brot** in the window and stands in line after Eli’s quiet “Wir warten.”
26. **Listen before speaking.** The baker hands over a roll to the previous customer: “Bitte schön.” Eli gestures *here you are*. Player hears transactional German before it is theirs to use.
27. **Read the known words.** On labels player finds **Brot**, **Brötchen**, and **Wasser**. Unknown words remain scenery; noticing familiar print is the target.
28. **Rehearse an order.** Eli whispers “Ein Brot, bitte.” Player echoes if desired. The phrase is a lifeline, not a performance requirement.
29. **Order turn and repair.** Baker looks at the player. “Ein Brot, bitte” succeeds; “Brot” prompts the helpful question “Ein Brot?” and permits an immediate repair rather than failure.
30. **Price and payment.** “Zwei Euro zwanzig, bitte” appears on the register and in sound. Player hands over ten euros, hears **Danke**, receives change, and need only recognise *zwei Euro* today.
31. **Leave the bakery.** Baker says “Schönen Tag noch.” Player uses **Tschüss**; Eli explains the farewell afterward so the counter never becomes a lecture hall.

### Nachmittag — supermarket, return, and first independent recall

32. **Supermarket threshold.** Automatic doors, trolley rattle, and **Obst** signage make the space louder. “Der Supermarkt” is named once; the player selects **Korb oder Wagen?**, reusing *oder* from breakfast.
33. **Find water.** Player scans until **Wasser** appears on an aisle sign. Eli waits three seconds before giving help, preserving retrieval.
34. **Still or sparkling.** Labels say *still* and *mit Kohlensäure*. Eli only shakes one bottle lightly; the player’s preference is theirs, not a vocabulary score.
35. **Three red apples.** At fruit, Eli says “rot”; player chooses **drei Äpfel**, weighs them at a screen with the apple picture, and sees plural print become a task.
36. **Find the checkout.** Player sees **Kasse** on a sign, follows it, and has used reading for navigation rather than a waypoint.
37. **Cashier greeting and bag.** “Hallo” is answered without Eli. “Eine Tüte?” is understood from the bags behind the counter; a choice scaffold is available, not imposed.
38. **Cash, receipt, rain.** Cashier says “Das macht …,” player pays, takes **den Bon**, and steps into light rain. **Regen** moves from radio noise into wet jacket and hood.
39. **Red, green, home.** At the crossing Eli says **rot/grün** while the player waits then walks. The façade number 27 and “Hier” return them home without a quest arrow.

### Abend — settling, memory, and the first quiet victory

40. **Unlock without prompt.** Eli remains a step behind while player opens **die Tür** with **dem Schlüssel**. The Day 1 symbol is already beginning to become invisible competence.
41. **Put shopping away.** **Wasser**, **Brot**, and **Äpfel** are named/matched as they enter cupboard, counter, and bowl. Repetition occurs through domestic order.
42. **Mara’s check-in.** “Alles gut?” Player answers **Ja, gut** or a supported “ein bisschen müde.” The emotional answer is allowed to be less than perfect German.
43. **Three apples again.** Mara says “Drei Äpfel” while the player places them in a bowl. Number, food, and plural return as shared satisfaction.
44. **Room recall sweep.** Back upstairs, player identifies **Bett, Fenster, Lampe, Tür, Schlüssel** in the actual room. There is no timer and no penalty; it is a quiet retrieval moment.
45. **Message home.** Player records/selects “Ich bin in Deutschland. Mir geht es gut.” A simpler “Ich bin gut” receives supportive recasting rather than rejection.
46. **Tomorrow’s plan.** Eli asks, “Morgen Bäckerei?” Player says **ja/nein**. Either response is accepted; agency is more important than setting a fixed route.
47. **Lamp out.** “Die Lampe aus” links the morning light switch to an actionable evening phrase and quietly seeds later separable-verb logic.
48. **Good night from Mara.** A distant “Gute Nacht” prompts the player’s reply. The phrase is no longer alone in a vocabulary list; it belongs to this household.
49. **Night soundscape.** Tram brakes, rain gutter, and keyring settle into a city the player can now name in fragments. No new words are imposed; memory consolidates.
50. **Sleep.** The player returns the key to the desk beside the note and closes their eyes. Tomorrow’s first recall will ask for *Schlüssel*, *Hallo*, and the bakery formula in altered order.

**Day 1 total: 50 core micro-events.** Tiny automatic reinforcements—object glances, ambient speech, and optional echoes—occur within these events but do not count as extra levels. This keeps the prologue within the required 40–60 range while protecting a true beginner from overload.

## 5. Anatomy of Story Day 15 — “Der Zettel im Briefkasten”

**Pedagogical status:** early A1; bridge toward later A1/A2 independence, not an A2 claim.  
**Day promise:** The player completes a familiar morning with reduced help, interprets a neighbour’s written request, asks for and follows simple directions, buys a specific item, handles a small public misunderstanding, describes an immediate plan, and makes an evening arrangement.  
**New focus:** present-tense word order; common separable verbs (*aufstehen, einkaufen, mitbringen*); accusative in high-frequency chunks; time words; *möchten/können*; simple reasons with *weil* as a meaning-first chunk; familiar Perfekt recognition in small talk.  
**Recycling:** Day 1’s key, door, water, bread, numbers, greetings, money, colours, and rooms return with less prompting.  
**Emotional curve:** capable routine → uncertainty caused by a note → independent repair → invitation into community.

The weather is dry after rain. The player has slept in the room for two weeks; the space now contains a mug chosen at a flea market, an opened language notebook, and a plant that has not died. Eli is cycling to the library and will not accompany the errands. This is not abandonment: it is a planned chance to discover what already works. The numbered beats below are deliberately granular author notes; they are nested into **50 playable core micro-events** (for example, the breakfast sequence is one playable task with several observable beats). This preserves the required 40–60-task day while retaining the requested child-story-level detail.

### Morgen — a routine that now belongs to the player

1. **07:10, familiar alarm.** The phone says “Guten Morgen.” Player turns it off without labels; *aufstehen* appears in a small natural reminder only after they rise. Goal: retrieve routine language, not recognise a picture.
2. **Weather at the window.** The player opens the curtain and says/selects “Es ist sonnig.” Earlier **Fenster** returns; new weather phrase gains a visual anchor.
3. **Choose clothes.** A light jacket, not the raincoat, is selected. The calendar says “Mittwoch.” Player connects weather and choice without an explicit conditional lesson.
4. **Make the bed.** Eli’s earlier phrase “Das Bett” becomes “Ich mache das Bett.” The sentence is offered after the action as an optional repeat.
5. **Plant check.** The player notices dry soil. “Die Pflanze braucht Wasser,” Mara says from the doorway. Target: third-person present and **braucht**, understood through need.
6. **Fill watering can.** Player turns on the tap and retrieves **Wasser** independently. The familiar noun now supports an extended sentence.
7. **Kitchen greeting.** Mara says, “Morgen! Hast du gut geschlafen?” Player chooses or speaks “Ja, ich habe gut geschlafen.” This is a high-frequency Perfekt chunk, practised as social warmth rather than analysed.
8. **Mara’s clarification.** If the player says “Ja, gut schlafen,” Mara understands then naturally repeats “Ah, du hast gut geschlafen.” No red mark interrupts breakfast.
9. **Coffee choice.** “Möchtest du Kaffee?” Player answers “Nein, danke. Ich möchte Tee.” **möchte** upgrades the Day 1 yes/no exchange.
10. **Prepare breakfast.** Player retrieves bread, butter, cup, plate, knife. Objects are no longer individually named; one soft recall prompt appears only if the player hesitates.
11. **Set two places.** Mara asks, “Kannst du bitte zwei Teller holen?” Player carries two plates. **kannst du** becomes a useful request, numbers repeat in a new role.
12. **The empty jar.** There is no jam. Player says “Die Marmelade ist leer.” Mara replies, “Dann kaufen wir später welche.” This seeds accusative/plural as a future chunk, not a worksheet.
13. **Mailbox key.** Mara asks, “Hast du den Schlüssel?” Player checks pocket and answers “Ja, ich habe ihn.” The pronoun is functionally introduced through a known object; grammar label waits.
14. **Stairwell independence.** Eli is carrying bicycle panniers downstairs. They say, “Ich muss los. Bis später!” Player replies “Bis später” and watches Eli leave. The first conscious step-back occurs without drama.
15. **Mailbox discovery.** The player opens **den Briefkasten** with the key and finds a yellow note: “Hallo! Könntest du heute bitte Milch mitbringen? Ich bin bis 18 Uhr bei der Arbeit. Danke! — Mara.”
16. **Read for gist.** The player highlights familiar words: *Hallo, heute, Milch, danke, Mara*. Goal: tolerate unknown text and extract purpose without translating every word.
17. **Ask a question.** Player may open the note’s help layer once. It gives a visual timeline (today / 18:00 / work) and reads the request slowly. Eli is unavailable; the support does not provide a complete first-language rewrite unless accessibility mode is enabled.
18. **Reply note.** Player writes/selects “Ja, ich bringe Milch mit.” The future is present tense with time context; *mitbringen* is introduced as a practical whole.
19. **Pin the list.** Player adds milk to their shopping list beside apples and bread. *einkaufen* is heard in “Ich gehe einkaufen.”

### Vormittag — neighbourhood route and purposeful speech

20. **Street greeting variation.** Frau Bender is watering geraniums. She asks, “Na, alles klar?” Player understands it as an informal check-in from repeated social context and answers “Ja, danke. Und Ihnen?” or a simpler supported equivalent.
21. **Frau Bender’s repair.** She says, “Mir auch, danke,” and points to the sky: “Schönes Wetter heute.” Player says “Ja, sehr schön.” Weather now makes small talk possible.
22. **Kiosk sign.** A handwritten sign announces “Paket abholen.” Player recognises *abholen* later, but today only notices it as living language on the route.
23. **Crossing plan.** Roadworks block the usual crossing. A worker says, “Hier können Sie nicht rüber.” Player reads gesture and **nicht**; goal is comprehension under a mild disruption.
24. **Ask for directions.** Player approaches the worker: “Entschuldigung, wo ist der Supermarkt?” The phrase was unlocked through three earlier street sign scenes and is now independently produced.
25. **Direction listening.** Worker answers slowly enough to be fair: “Geradeaus, dann links an der Apotheke.” Player sees the street extend ahead; **geradeaus**, **links**, and **an der** are tied to landmarks.
26. **Confirm repair.** Player says, “Links an der Apotheke?” Worker nods, “Genau.” The game rewards clarification, not flawless first-pass listening.
27. **Follow the route.** The player walks straight, passes the pharmacy’s green cross, and turns left. Direction words are embodied and do not appear as floating arrows unless accessibility guidance is on.
28. **Unexpected call.** Mara messages: “Bitte auch Hafermilch, wenn es welche gibt.” Player hears *wenn es welche gibt* without being asked to generate it; the goal is notice availability and add an optional item.
29. **Supermarket entrance.** The automatic doors and basket return from Day 1. Player says “Ich brauche Milch und Hafermilch.” The need formula is now self-generated.
30. **Dairy aisle search.** Signs show **Milchprodukte**. Player uses the word family to infer likely location, a small reading strategy.
31. **Ask an employee.** The oat milk shelf is empty. Player says “Entschuldigung, haben Sie Hafermilch?” This is an authentic information request with a bounded answer.
32. **Employee response.** “Heute leider nicht. Vielleicht morgen wieder.” Player extracts *heute nicht* and *morgen*. No punitive failure: the world has stock problems.
33. **Alternative choice.** Player examines lactose-free milk and calls Mara, or sends a message: “Keine Hafermilch. Ist laktosefreie Milch okay?” Goal: turn a shopping failure into communicative negotiation.
34. **Mara replies.** “Ja, bitte. Danke dir!” Player understands affectionate **dir** as a socially meaningful thank-you, not a case chart.
35. **Compare prices.** Two bottles cost €1,29 and €2,09. Player chooses according to Mara’s modest household budget cue; numbers now carry responsibility.
36. **Checkout queue small talk.** A child drops a red glove. Player says “Hier, bitte” while returning it. Day 1’s **hier** and **bitte** now create a kind social action.
37. **Bag decision.** Cashier asks “Haben Sie eine Tasche dabei?” Player answers “Ja, ich habe eine Tasche.” The reusable bag from home turns a familiar question into fluent routine.
38. **Receipt check.** Player notices the receipt shows *Milch laktosefrei*. Reading is functional: they confirm the right substitute was purchased.

### Mittag — a social detour and language repair

39. **Café window invitation.** Outside, Samira waves from the café. The player has ten minutes before going home. They choose whether to enter; social agency matters.
40. **Order with preference.** Samira asks, “Was darf es sein?” Player says “Ein Wasser, bitte” or “Ich hätte gern einen Tee.” The latter is modelled as a more polite variant, not demanded.
41. **The wrong assumption.** Samira thinks the player says “Torte” rather than “Tee” over the espresso machine. She brings cake. The player sees the mismatch and must repair: “Entschuldigung, ich möchte Tee, keinen Kuchen.”
42. **Samira’s response.** “Ah, Tee! Natürlich.” She laughs at the noisy machine, never at the player, and exchanges the order. The social consequence is relief, not shame.
43. **Ask about work.** Player asks “Arbeitest du heute?” Samira says “Ja, bis drei.” Player’s familiar word **Arbeit** becomes a verb form by context.
44. **Simple reason.** Samira asks why the player is shopping. Player answers “Weil Mara arbeiten ist” or the more natural supported “weil Mara arbeitet.” The system accepts communicative early-A1 output, recasts gently, and stores **weil** for richer use.
45. **Invitation seed.** Samira says, “Heute Abend sind ein paar Leute im Park. Kommst du?” Player responds “Vielleicht. Ich muss erst nach Hause.” Modal meaning enters through a real scheduling conflict.
46. **Time negotiation.** “Um sieben?” Samira asks. Player repeats “Um sieben.” Clock language is practised as a commitment, not a number list.
47. **Farewell variation.** Instead of **Tschüss**, Samira says “Bis dann.” Player chooses it back. The lexicon becomes socially textured.

### Nachmittag — home responsibility and a first past-time story

48. **Return route alone.** Eli remains absent. Player chooses the detour past the pharmacy based on the worker’s directions; route confidence is evaluated by world navigation, not a vocabulary quiz.
49. **Door and key recall.** Player uses their key without prompt. The Day 1 anxiety object has become invisible competence.
50. **Kitchen delivery.** Mara is not home. Player puts milk in the fridge and leaves a note: “Milch ist im Kühlschrank.” The compound returns as a readable, useful word.
51. **Substitute explanation.** Second note: “Keine Hafermilch. Ich habe laktosefreie Milch gekauft.” Player produces or selects a clear explanation. The phrase is later reused in a pharmacy and a workplace substitution problem.
52. **Dishwasher sound.** The dishwasher beeps. A sticky note says “Kannst du sie ausräumen?” Player infers task from visible clean dishes and action; **ausräumen** is encountered as a whole verb.
53. **Sort items.** Player puts plates, cups, and cutlery away. Previously known **Teller** and **Tasse** return in plural-rich context; exact endings are noticed through audio, not forced yet.
54. **Mara’s voice note.** She thanks the player: “Du hast mir wirklich geholfen.” Player hears a natural Perfekt form tied to a real contribution.
55. **Reply with a past event.** Player records “Ich habe keine Hafermilch gefunden.” This is constrained, meaningful Perfekt production; sentence-building support fades after three attempts.
56. **Laundry decision.** A full washing basket invites action, but the player has park plans. They say “Ich kann heute nicht waschen. Ich wasche morgen.” Modal, negation, and future-by-present appear in a realistic choice.
57. **Check the clock.** It is 17:35. Player says “Es ist fünf Uhr fünfunddreißig” or uses digital support. Time is now social punctuality.
58. **Change clothes.** The player chooses a warmer layer because evening will be cooler. An in-world forecast says “Am Abend wird es kalt.” This is rich input without a production test.

### Abend — park belonging, not performance

59. **Park entrance.** The grassy path opens to a small group around a blanket. Samira calls, “Da bist du ja!” Player recognises a welcoming idiom through gesture and tone.
60. **Introduce self again.** New NPC Leonie says “Hi, ich bin Leonie.” Player answers “Ich heiße … Ich wohne in der Lindenstraße.” The Day 1 introduction has grown one factual branch.
61. **Offer food.** Tarek holds out a bowl: “Möchtest du Chips?” Player says yes/no with **möchte**, then offers someone their bread roll. Reciprocity extends transactional language.
62. **Name objects in play.** A child asks “Wo ist der Ball?” Player spots it near a bench and says “Da!” then “Der Ball ist da.” Spatial reference becomes spontaneous.
63. **Weather comment.** Leonie says, “Zum Glück regnet es nicht.” Player replies, “Ja, heute ist es schön.” Day 1 rain and Day 15 sun form a tiny weather memory.
64. **Misunderstood joke.** Tarek says a simple wordplay about **Bank** (bench/bank). Player need not laugh. They can ask, “Was bedeutet das?” The game normalises curiosity around humour.
65. **Explanation without lecture.** Tarek points to the bench and mimes money. Player understands that one word can have two meanings; no test follows.
66. **Express preference.** Samira asks, “Magst du Neustadt?” Player answers “Ja, ich mag den Park / die Bäckerei / meine Wohnung.” Accusative forms appear through frequent chunks; the player’s actual taste matters.
67. **One honest difficulty.** Leonie asks, “Ist Deutsch schwer?” Player can say “Ja, ein bisschen. Aber ich lerne jeden Tag.” This scene protects beginner vulnerability and gives it a voice.
68. **Group response.** No one applauds like a classroom. Samira says, “Das merkt man.” Tarek offers, “Du kannst uns immer fragen.” The player feels included, not assessed.
69. **Leave politely.** At 20:15, player says “Ich gehe jetzt nach Hause. Bis morgen!” A reason is optional: “weil ich müde bin.” The player controls their boundary.
70. **Walk with Leonie.** Leonie walks partway and tells a short, slow story: “Gestern habe ich meinen Bus verpasst.” Player is asked only “Oh nein—und dann?” which supports listening through interest rather than grammar extraction.
71. **Goodnight to Mara.** At home Mara has returned. Player says “Die Milch ist im Kühlschrank.” Mara reads the note and says “Perfekt, danke.” The day’s errand receives real acknowledgment.
72. **Recap choice.** Before bed, the player chooses three moments for a visual memory strip: note, worker, café repair, park. Each replays the player’s own German line at reduced support tomorrow, creating personal spaced repetition.
73. **Language journal.** Player writes two optional sentences: “Heute habe ich Milch gekauft. Heute Abend war ich im Park.” The journal preserves imperfect output and later reveals growth; it is never graded for public display.
74. **Lights out.** The familiar “Gute Nacht” is now offered to Mara first. The player turns off the lamp, hears city rain gutters dripping, and schedules the milk words for Day 16’s breakfast and Day 22’s pharmacy scene.

**Day 15 total: 50 core micro-events, expressed through 74 granular authored beats.** The core tasks are: morning routine (1–14), interpreting and answering the note (15–19), route repair (20–28), shopping and substitution (29–38), café repair and arrangement (39–47), household follow-through (48–58), and park belonging/reflection (59–74). Standard interstitial days remain 40–60 core tasks. The player leaves Day 15 as a genuine early-A1 speaker: still slow, still supported, but no longer silent inside ordinary life.

## 8. Assessment without breaking immersion

No character says, “You have completed the dative.” Instead, language capability is evidenced by life capability. The game tracks four separate channels:

| Capability | In-world evidence | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Recognition** | notices a sign, sound, or phrase in context | spots that a platform has changed before missing the train |
| **Comprehension** | chooses an action that shows intended meaning | understands “bitte warten” and stays at the clinic desk |
| **Production** | says/writes a usable message | orders a substitute product and explains why |
| **Repair and flexibility** | responds when first attempt fails | corrects “Torte” to “Tee” without collapsing into silence |

Progress gate scenes offer several credible routes. A shy player may send a voice note before making a call; an advanced player may speak freely. Accessibility options include slower speech, captions, visual vocabulary support, replay, and translation access. These preserve learning dignity; they do not lower narrative stakes. High-stakes scenes always offer a clear-language document layer and no irreversible punishment.

## 9. Tone, cultural care, and narrative boundaries

Neustadt is not a museum of stereotypes. Germany is shown through regional variation, generational differences, multilingual households, disability access, mixed class positions, queer and immigrant lives, public systems, bureaucracy, humour, quiet rules, and contradiction. No NPC exists simply to correct the player. Every person has an errand, preference, bad day, and capacity for kindness.

The script distinguishes directness from rudeness, formality from coldness, and efficiency from lack of care. It avoids claiming that all Germans behave one way. Dialect appears as optional listening flavour with context and subtitles, never as an obstacle designed to mock a learner. Cultural scenes invite observation, not tests of belonging. The protagonist never needs to erase their first language or history to succeed; their growing German adds a self rather than replacing one.

## 10. Final image and reusable authoring rule

On Day 360, the player returns to Lindenhof 27 after the capstone event. The same stairwell holds its bicycle, cardboard, and dim afternoon light. A new tenant stands on the landing with a suitcase, an envelope, and a key they do not yet trust. They are staring at the wrong door.

The player may choose to say, slowly, “Hallo. Ich heiße [name]. Kann ich dir helfen?” The new arrival tries to answer. The player waits. They do not fill the silence. They point only if asked. The game cuts not on a certificate, but on an open door.

**Authoring rule for every future micro-event:** if an action could happen in a person’s actual day, give it a sensory context, a human reason, a language function, a possible misunderstanding, and a later return. The player should never feel that life pauses so German can begin. German is the life they are building.
